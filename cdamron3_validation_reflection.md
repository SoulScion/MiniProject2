#### MiniProject3 - Validation Reflection

For MiniProject3, me and my teammate independently validated each other's ten github projects and then came together to discuss disagreements in validation before finally taking the resolutions and making statistics out of them.

In regards to patterns seen within the both of our data, most of the disagreements we had were in regards to the "ActivityPattern" and "RecentThemes" classifications, which shows that often it is a little more difficult or subjective to categorize the trend within a graph of commits and the most recent 10 commits within a specific repository. While a graph of commits can have trend lines that depicte the usually expected acending, decending, or stable history, github repositories tend to be significantly more eratic than other graphs. Due to the way commits work on github, where many different contributors can make their own commits to the master branch all within relatively the similar time frames, the change in number of commits every 2 to 3 months can be a huge jump. These attributes of commit history combine to make a realtively unstable graph that can sometimes display an overall trend or no trend at all. In regards to "RecentThemes" though, the problem arises with the number of commits being researched. Often when analyzing data, the most basic principle is "the more the data, the better" and that exact philosophy is why 10 commits is not enough to really evaluate the current themes within the most recent information. The only other reason disagreements arose for themes is becuase the limited number of labels or categories didn't really help with giving a value to what me and my partner thought was actually going on in the most recent 10 commits.

Moving on from our disagreements over validation though, another unqiue pattern to the data was that recovery after a gap was often associated with maitenance needs and/or updates being worked on by the original maintainers as well as renewed development by a different party. This suggests that what causes github repositories to stay active the longest is their need for constant, large updates and bug fixes to keep up with the users of the program or a desire to rework an older program by newer people for their specific uses. In accordance with the trends above, the decline or disappearance of activity on a repository didn't always guarentee its death forever due to how recovery worked. Even though some repositories required constant work throughout many years, what happened more regularly in the data we analyzed was a sort of dormant or low activity period and then a update or fix period, followed by another dormant-like period. While many repositories we researched tended to slowly decend as part of their trends, most other graphs that were decending followed certain irregular periods of dormant-like states to active-like states.

Overall, this validation mini-series of projects displayed how large amounts of knowledge on a repository can be gleaned from commit history alone, however, that knowledge is often imperfect and/or based upon a loose amount of assumptions that don't always add up to being true in the end. This project also provided the insight that having a partner come in to challenge the established values a person gave to data is always benefical in making sure that research into digital traces is as close to truth as it can be, based upon the data given.
